The 3-check ski day

A 30-second look at cheeks, fingers, toes and mood.

  1. Before the first liftFace covered, goggles meet the helmet, one dry sock, mittens on.
  2. At every warm-up breakLook at cheeks and noses, feel fingers and toes, swap anything wet.
  3. After lunchQuiet, clumsy or cranky? Make it a shorter afternoon.

The numbers pediatric guidance uses

  • 5°FAt this temperature or wind chill and colder, cut back time outside CPS
  • -15°FBelow this, avoid outdoor play; exposed skin begins to freeze within minutes AAP
  • -19°F0°F air with a 15 mph wind feels like this; frostbite can happen in about 30 minutes NWS
  • 95°FA body temperature below this is a hypothermia emergency: get medical help CDC
Spot it early

What to look for, and what to do

Little kids often can't tell you they're cold. Look, don't ask. Most of what you'll see is early and fixable in the lodge.

Frostnip

Look for
Red, stinging or tingly cheeks, nose, ears or fingers.
Do
Go inside, cover the skin and warm it gently. Don't rub.
Read the guide

Possible frostbite

Look for
White, grey or waxy skin that's firm and numb.
Do
Get help from ski patrol or the clinic. No heaters, no rubbing.
Read the guide

Getting too cold

Look for
Shivering, clumsy hands, stumbling, unusually quiet.
Do
Head in now: dry layers, warm drink, warm the core.
Read the guide

Call 911 or patrol

Look for
Confused, very sleepy, slurred words, or stops shivering while still cold.
Do
This is an emergency. Keep them warm while help comes.
Read the guide

Altitude

Look for
Headache, nausea, not hungry, fussy or poor sleep after arriving.
Do
Rest, eat, go no higher today. Get care if it's getting worse.
Read the guide

Wet gear

Look for
Soaked mittens, snow in cuffs, damp base layer.
Do
Swap to dry spares at the next break. Wet is how kids get cold.
Read the guide

Cold-safety guides for ski families

Grounded in CDC, AAP, National Weather Service, Wilderness Medical Society and children's hospital guidance, with every number linked to its source.

Cold skinFrostnip & frostbiteFrostnip is common, and it's easy to catch early. Two minutes of looking at cheeks, noses and fingers at every warm-up break keeps a cold morning from turning into frostbite.Whole-body coldHypothermia in young kidsHypothermia on a ski day usually builds slowly: wet layers, a long lesson, a windy lift. The early signs are easy to see once you know them, and dry clothes plus a warm lodge fix most of it.Wind chillHow cold is too coldThere's no single number every resort uses, but pediatric guidance gives you a clear line. Check the wind chill, not just the temperature, and plan the day around it.AltitudeAltitude + cold prepMost kids do great at altitude. A slower first day, a night lower down if you can, and knowing the few signs to watch for are all it takes.GearToddler gear checklistDressing a toddler for the snow comes down to three things: thin layers you can add or take off, nothing wet against the skin, and every bit of skin covered when the wind picks up. Here's the whole list, from car seat to chairlift.GearHand & foot warmersHand and toe warmers are a great extra on a cold ski day, and the Wilderness Medical Society lists them as one way to keep fingers and toes warm. They just need a few rules with kids: always a layer of fabric between warmer and skin, never in a boot that's already snug, and never as a fix for skin that's already numb.
