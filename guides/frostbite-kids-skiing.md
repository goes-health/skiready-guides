# Frostnip & Frostbite in Kids Skiing: Cheeks, Fingers, Toes & Early Signs

> How to spot frostnip early on your child's cheeks, nose, fingers and toes while skiing, what to do in the lodge, what not to do, and when to get care. Based on CDC, AAP and Wilderness Medical Society guidance.

Source page: https://skiready.goes.health/guides/frostbite-kids-skiing
Updated: 2026-11-27

Frostnip is common, and it's easy to catch early. Two minutes of looking at cheeks, noses and fingers at every warm-up break keeps a cold morning from turning into frostbite.

## Quick answer
1. Red, stinging skin is the early stage (frostnip). Go inside and warm it gently.
2. White, grey or waxy, firm, numb skin can be frostbite. Get help from ski patrol or a clinic.
3. Never rub it, and never use a fire, heater or heating pad to warm it.

Sources: [Canadian Paediatric Society: Frostbite](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/safety-and-injury-prevention/frostbite), [CDC: Preventing frostbite](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/prevention/preventing-frostbite.html)

## Where it starts on kids

Frostbite usually hits the nose, ears, cheeks, chin, fingers and toes. ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/prevention/preventing-frostbite.html)) On a chairlift the face takes the wind, and small kids barely move their faces, so cheeks and noses are where you'll see it first. Babies and toddlers are at higher risk because they move less and can't tell you they're cold. ([CPS](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/safety-and-injury-prevention/frostbite)) So look, don't ask.

## Symptom check: frostnip vs. frostbite

| What you see | What it likely is | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Red, slightly swollen skin that stings, burns or tingles | Frostnip (early stage) | Go inside, cover the skin, warm it gently. It should feel normal again as it warms ([CPS](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/safety-and-injury-prevention/frostbite)) |
| Tingling skin that's starting to look grey | Getting worse | Get indoors now and warm it gently with body heat or warm (not hot) water ([CPS](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/safety-and-injury-prevention/frostbite)) |
| White, grayish-yellow or waxy patch that feels firm and numb | Possible frostbite | Get medical help: ski patrol, the resort clinic, or 911 if they're also showing signs of hypothermia ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/prevention/preventing-frostbite.html)) |
| Numbness that doesn't go away after warming | Needs a medical check | Get medical advice right away ([CPS](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/safety-and-injury-prevention/frostbite)) |

## Warming up in the lodge: do and don't

**Do**
- Get out of the wind and into the lodge. Swap wet mittens, socks and neck gaiters for dry ones. ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/prevention/preventing-frostbite.html))
- Warm the skin gently: a warm hand over cheeks, fingers tucked into an armpit, or warm (not hot) water. ([CPS](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/safety-and-injury-prevention/frostbite))
- Take off rings or anything tight on the hand or foot before it swells. ([WMS](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/10806032231222359))
- Check for hypothermia signs too: shivering, clumsiness, unusual quiet or sleepiness. ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/prevention/index.html))

**Don't**
- Don't rub or massage the skin, and never rub it with snow. ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/prevention/preventing-frostbite.html)) ([WMS](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/10806032231222359))
- Don't warm it with a fireplace, heat lamp, radiator, stove, heating pad or electric blanket. Numb skin burns easily. ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/prevention/preventing-frostbite.html))
- Don't walk on frostbitten feet or toes unless you have to. ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/prevention/preventing-frostbite.html))
- Don't thaw it if it could freeze again on the way down. Refreezing does more damage. ([WMS](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/10806032231222359))

## Fit and packing tips that prevent most frostnip

- One thin wool or synthetic ski sock, not two. Boots should let toes wiggle; tight boots cut circulation. ([CPS](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/safety-and-injury-prevention/frostbite))
- Mittens over gloves for little hands, so fingers stay together and share warmth. Pack a spare dry pair. ([CPS](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/safety-and-injury-prevention/frostbite))
- Face covered on cold or windy days: a balaclava or neck gaiter plus goggles that seal against the helmet. ([SickKids](https://www.aboutkidshealth.ca/cold-weather-injuries))
- Layers you can add or take off quickly, and nothing cotton next to the skin. ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/english/safety-prevention/at-play/pages/winter-safety.aspx))
- Warm-up breaks indoors. Kids having fun don't notice they're getting cold. ([AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/english/safety-prevention/at-play/pages/winter-safety.aspx))

## When it's more than frostnip

If the skin stays white, grey, waxy or numb after warming, or blisters show up, get medical help the same day. Resort clinics and ski patrol treat frostbite often. When the closest care is more than 2 hours away, the Wilderness Medical Society describes rewarming in water held at 37 to 39°C (98.6 to 102.2°F), but only if the part won't freeze again. ([WMS](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/10806032231222359)) At a ski resort, care is almost always closer than that, so head to patrol or the clinic.

## Parent FAQ

**What does frostnip look like on a child's cheeks?**
Red, slightly swollen skin that stings or burns, sometimes tingly. It's the early stage and usually gets better with gentle rewarming indoors (Canadian Paediatric Society).

**How fast can kids get frostbite while skiing?**
It depends on the wind chill. On the National Weather Service chart, exposed skin can get frostbite in 30 minutes at about -19°F wind chill (0°F air with a 15 mph wind), and faster as it gets colder. Chairlift rides and fast runs add wind.

**Should I rub my child's cold hands to warm them?**
No. Don't rub or massage frostbitten skin, and don't use a fire, heater or heating pad. Warm it gently with body heat or warm (not hot) water (CDC).

**When should I take my child to a doctor for frostbite?**
If the skin is white, grey, waxy, firm or numb, or still numb after warming, get medical help. Call 911 if your child also shows signs of hypothermia, like confusion, drowsiness or clumsiness.
