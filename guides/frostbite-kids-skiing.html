Frostnip & Frostbite in Kids Skiing: Cheeks, Fingers, Toes & Early Signs

Frostnip is common, and it's easy to catch early. Two minutes of looking at cheeks, noses and fingers at every warm-up break keeps a cold morning from turning into frostbite.

Updated Nov 26, 2026 · Sources: CDC, CPS, AAP, SickKids, WMS, NWS

Quick answer

  1. Red, stinging skin is the early stage (frostnip). Go inside and warm it gently.
  2. White, grey or waxy, firm, numb skin can be frostbite. Get help from ski patrol or a clinic.
  3. Never rub it, and never use a fire, heater or heating pad to warm it.

CPSCDC

Where it starts on kids

Frostbite usually hits the nose, ears, cheeks, chin, fingers and toes. CDC On a chairlift the face takes the wind, and small kids barely move their faces, so cheeks and noses are where you'll see it first. Babies and toddlers are at higher risk because they move less and can't tell you they're cold. CPS So look, don't ask.

Warming up in the lodge: do and don't

Do

  • Get out of the wind and into the lodge. Swap wet mittens, socks and neck gaiters for dry ones. CDC
  • Warm the skin gently: a warm hand over cheeks, fingers tucked into an armpit, or warm (not hot) water. CPS
  • Take off rings or anything tight on the hand or foot before it swells. WMS
  • Check for hypothermia signs too: shivering, clumsiness, unusual quiet or sleepiness. CDC

Don't

  • Don't rub or massage the skin, and never rub it with snow. CDC WMS
  • Don't warm it with a fireplace, heat lamp, radiator, stove, heating pad or electric blanket. Numb skin burns easily. CDC
  • Don't walk on frostbitten feet or toes unless you have to. CDC
  • Don't thaw it if it could freeze again on the way down. Refreezing does more damage. WMS

Fit and packing tips that prevent most frostnip

  • One thin wool or synthetic ski sock, not two. Boots should let toes wiggle; tight boots cut circulation. CPS
  • Mittens over gloves for little hands, so fingers stay together and share warmth. Pack a spare dry pair. CPS
  • Face covered on cold or windy days: a balaclava or neck gaiter plus goggles that seal against the helmet. SickKids
  • Layers you can add or take off quickly, and nothing cotton next to the skin. AAP
  • Warm-up breaks indoors. Kids having fun don't notice they're getting cold. AAP

When it's more than frostnip

If the skin stays white, grey, waxy or numb after warming, or blisters show up, get medical help the same day. Resort clinics and ski patrol treat frostbite often. When the closest care is more than 2 hours away, the Wilderness Medical Society describes rewarming in water held at 37 to 39°C (98.6 to 102.2°F), but only if the part won't freeze again. WMS At a ski resort, care is almost always closer than that, so head to patrol or the clinic.

Parent FAQ

What does frostnip look like on a child's cheeks?

Red, slightly swollen skin that stings or burns, sometimes tingly. It's the early stage and usually gets better with gentle rewarming indoors (Canadian Paediatric Society).

How fast can kids get frostbite while skiing?

It depends on the wind chill. On the National Weather Service chart, exposed skin can get frostbite in 30 minutes at about -19°F wind chill (0°F air with a 15 mph wind), and faster as it gets colder. Chairlift rides and fast runs add wind.

Should I rub my child's cold hands to warm them?

No. Don't rub or massage frostbitten skin, and don't use a fire, heater or heating pad. Warm it gently with body heat or warm (not hot) water (CDC).

When should I take my child to a doctor for frostbite?

If the skin is white, grey, waxy, firm or numb, or still numb after warming, get medical help. Call 911 if your child also shows signs of hypothermia, like confusion, drowsiness or clumsiness.
